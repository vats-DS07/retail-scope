Cleaning and visualizing two years of online retail

A complete data-science workflow on the UCI Online Retail II dataset: over one million real transactions from a UK-based online gift wholesaler. The raw Excel file is cleaned with Pandas, explored with descriptive statistics, and every chart on this site is produced by Matplotlib.

◎Project objectives

  • Load and inspect a large, real-world transactional dataset.
  • Find and fix data-quality problems: duplicates, missing values, inconsistent names, cancellations and outliers.
  • Create useful derived variables such as revenue and date parts.
  • Describe sales, products, customers and countries with descriptive statistics.
  • Communicate the results with clear Matplotlib charts and a static website.
  • Turn the analysis into practical business recommendations.

Cleaning operations, step by step

    How cancellations and returns were treated

    • Invoices starting with "C" are cancellations or returns. They are kept with negative quantity and negative revenue, so adding up revenue gives true net revenue.
    • Negative quantities without "C" have a price of £0 and notes like "damaged" or "missing". They are internal stock write-offs, labelled Stock Adjustment, and left out of sales figures.
    • Invoices starting with "A" are bad-debt accounting entries (6 rows) and are excluded from sales.
    • Postage, fees and manual codes (POST, DOT, M, AMAZONFEE...) are flagged IsProduct = False so they do not distort product rankings.

    Exploratory Data Analysis

    Statistics are computed with Pandas in analysis/exploratory_analysis.py. Every chart below is a PNG produced by Matplotlib from the cleaned data. Revenue figures are net (sales minus cancellations) for product lines only. Click a chart to enlarge it.

    Statistical summary

    pandas .describe() + skewness

    Every variable has a mean far above its median and a large positive skewness. The data is strongly right-skewed, so medians and log-scaled charts describe a "typical" order better than averages.

    Correlation matrix (customer level)

    Pearson measures straight-line relationships and is sensitive to extreme values; Spearman uses ranks and is more reliable for skewed data like this. Compare both to see the effect of outliers.

    Insights & Conclusion

    Each finding below is generated from the analysis results, so the numbers always match the charts.

    Major findings

    ↗Important trends

      ✧Interesting discoveries

      • The two Excel sheets overlap for 1-9 December 2010; without spotting this, that period would be counted twice.
      • The two largest orders in the data (80,995 and 74,215 units) were cancelled within minutes; keeping cancellations as negative revenue makes them cancel out automatically.
      • The shop trades on Sundays but almost never on Saturdays.

      ✓Business recommendations

      Prepare early for the Q4 peak

      Build stock of best-selling Christmas and home-decor lines by August, because revenue rises sharply from September and peaks in November.

      Protect top customers

      About a fifth of customers bring in roughly three-quarters of revenue. A loyalty or account-manager programme for them is the safest way to protect income.

      Win back one-time buyers

      Over a quarter of customers ordered only once. Follow-up offers to first-time buyers could raise repeat-purchase rates.

      Grow export markets

      Overseas customers place much larger orders. Ireland, the Netherlands, Germany and France are proven markets worth targeted marketing.

      Investigate cancellations

      About one in six invoices is a cancellation. Review the most-cancelled products for quality, description or delivery problems.

      Record customer IDs

      Almost a quarter of rows have no Customer ID. Requiring an account or email at checkout would make customer analysis more complete.

      !Limitations

      • The data comes from a single UK retailer, so results may not apply to other businesses.
      • December 2011 is incomplete (data ends on 9 December), so it is left out of year-on-year comparisons.
      • Customer-level results only include rows with a Customer ID.
      • No cost or profit data is available, so the analysis is about revenue, not profitability.
      • Exact duplicate rows were removed; a few could have been genuine repeated scans.
      • The analysis is descriptive. It shows patterns and associations, not causes.

      About the Project

      Project description

      RetailScope is a Foundation of Data Science capstone that takes a large, messy, real-world sales file and turns it into a clean dataset, a set of statistical findings and an easy-to-use website. The project shows the full workflow a data scientist follows: understanding the data, cleaning it carefully and honestly, exploring it, visualising it and communicating the results.

      Objectives

      • Apply Pandas for real data cleaning and transformation.
      • Use descriptive statistics to summarise business data.
      • Create clear, labelled charts with Matplotlib.
      • Present the work in a static website without any framework.

      Dataset source

      Chen, D. (2012). Online Retail II [Dataset]. UCI Machine Learning Repository. https://doi.org/10.24432/C5CG6D

      All transactions between 01/12/2009 and 09/12/2011 for a UK-based, registered, non-store online retailer that mainly sells unique all-occasion giftware. Many customers are wholesalers. Licensed under CC BY 4.0.

      Methodology

      1. Load both Excel sheets with pd.read_excel.
      2. Profile the data: shape, types, missing values, duplicates.
      3. Clean: remove overlap and duplicates, fix types and names, classify transactions, flag outliers.
      4. Derive Revenue and date features; export the cleaned CSV.
      5. Analyse with groupby, pivot tables, quantiles, correlation and Pareto analysis.
      6. Plot with Matplotlib, export PNGs and a small data.js file for the website.
